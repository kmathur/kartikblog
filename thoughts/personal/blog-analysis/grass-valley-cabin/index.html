In My Own Company: My Solo Trip to Grass Valley

In My Own Company: My Solo Trip to Grass Valley

Grass Valley lies in the western foothills of the Sierra Nevada, about an hour northeast of Sacramento, where the flat Central Valley begins to rise into pine and oak. This is Gold Country. Highway 49 threads through old mining towns, Nevada City is four miles up the road, and the Yuba River runs through the canyons to the north. At around 2,500 feet, the air is cooler and the trees are taller than anything in the valley below.

Beyond the towns, at the end of a quiet road, a small cabin sits among the trees. It is a tiny home that holds what you need and very little else. Freddie and I had it to ourselves.

Silence was the first thing I noticed. In the beginning it felt almost loud, the way a room does just after the music stops. After a while I stopped noticing it and started resting in it.

The days found their own shape. I cleared a little work and a few tasks I had been carrying around. I wandered through the bookstores in town and read for hours. I walked and hiked in the cold, clean air. I found new music and played it on a loop. And I wrote, more than I have in years, the kind of creative writing I kept pushing to some later date.

Loose as the days were, they still had discipline. I didn't drink, and I found I didn't need to: simple food and my own company were enough to unwind. I barely spoke to anyone, I stayed away from the endless scroll, and when my mind tried to run its usual worried circles, I let them pass.

That left room for the harder work. I let myself stay with the things that have been weighing on me, without rushing to fix them, and thought about where my life stands and where I want it to go. Distance helped. Seen from a cabin in the woods, the same problems had clearer edges, and I came away knowing more about what I want next.

Freddie found his corner early and kept it. From my seat above, I could look down on him curled up there, and few things made me happier. He seemed entirely at ease, as if he understood that I wasn't going anywhere and neither was he.

What I valued most was how unhurried the days felt. For once, the clock was working for me, and each day was mine to arrange. I ate ramen for dinner, again for breakfast, and once more for brunch, simply because I felt like it. That small freedom felt enormous.

It also made something plain. Much of the friction in ordinary life comes from sharing it. That is no one's fault. The people around us have their own rhythms and plans, and they can never want exactly what we want at the moment we want it. At home and at work we negotiate constantly: what to do today, who gets which space, whose turn it is with the chores. Alone, there is nothing to negotiate. Barring an accident, it is hard for a solo trip to go wrong, and maybe that is what a real rest looks like.
